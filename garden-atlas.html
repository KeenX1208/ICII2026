About the Team

Team BWYA is made up of students in Grades 9 to 11 from different schools, cities, and backgrounds. We have different interests, strengths, and ways of looking at problems, but we came together with a simple idea: to build something meaningful and see how far we could take it. For us, iGEM is not just a competition or a research project. It is a chance to sit around the same table, argue about experiments, redesign ideas, interview people, learn unexpected skills, and turn conversations into a real project. We do not expect one student project to change the world, but we believe small things matter. If our work makes one problem clearer, pushes one idea further, or makes someone else curious, it is already worthwhile. Perhaps the biggest change happens to us: we learn that science is not only in laboratories or textbooks, but something people do together.

Project Description

Our project explores synthetic biology for postharvest preservation by engineering E. coli to produce melatonin. Harvested fruits, vegetables, and flowers remain biologically active and undergo senescence, water loss, stress, and microbial deterioration, causing substantial postharvest loss. We chose cut flowers as a model because their senescence is rapid, visible, and quantifiable through vase life, wilting, and appearance. Melatonin, involved in plant stress responses and senescence regulation, may improve postharvest quality. We engineered E. coli through two generations. Melatonin 1.0 established the heterologous pathway and identified constraints such as precursor availability, hydroxylation cofactors, and competition from native tryptophan metabolism. Melatonin 2.0 redesigned the pathway into a modular multi-gene system, strengthened cofactor synthesis and regeneration, and deleted trpR, tnaA, and yddG to redirect tryptophan flux. We used HPLC to evaluate melatonin production and tested a cell-removed, non-living fermentation product on cut flowers. Cut flowers are not the endpoint, but a model for broader postharvest agriculture, connecting metabolic engineering, microbial fermentation, and supply-chain needs to reduce loss and improve resource efficiency.

Cultural Connection

Flowers have long carried meanings far beyond decoration in Chinese culture. Plum blossoms symbolize resilience, orchids refinement, chrysanthemums integrity, and peonies prosperity. Flowers appear throughout poetry, painting, festivals, gardens, and gift-giving, acting as carriers of emotion, memory, and symbolism. Yet their beauty is temporary, and their short blooming period has become a recurring theme in Chinese literature and aesthetics, reflecting the tension between appreciating a moment and watching it disappear. Our project connects with this tradition not by reproducing an ancient preservation technique, but by reinterpreting the idea of extending the meaningful life of flowers through modern biotechnology. We engineer microorganisms to produce melatonin and test whether it can delay postharvest senescence in cut flowers, bringing a modern biological perspective to an old human desire: keeping flowers fresh for a little longer. The project also reflects a deeply rooted cultural idea: making full use of resources and avoiding unnecessary waste. Flowers require land, water, energy, transportation, and labor before reaching consumers; when they deteriorate rapidly, much of that investment is lost. By using cut flowers as a model for postharvest agriculture, we explore how synthetic biology might help reduce such losses. For us, culture is not only inherited from the past; it also shapes the questions we ask today. Traditional appreciation of flowers, respect for their seasonal life cycle, and the value placed on using resources carefully provide a cultural lens through which we rethink postharvest preservation with synthetic biology.

CAU-China

About the Team

We are Team CAU-China from China Agricultural University, composed of 20 members majoring in biology, mathematics, and veterinary medicine, united by the belief that synthetic biology can offer better solutions. This year, we are tackling the problem of food mold—combining rigorous experimental design with compassionate, people-centered human practices. We listen to scientists, farmers, and consumers to ensure our work addresses real-world needs while aligning with the Sustainable Development Goals. Our project, Fungal Sentinel, is a yeast GPCR-based early mold detection biosensor, and we hope it will inspire new ways to safeguard our food, our health, and our future.

Project Description

The global annual loss of food due to mold is approximately 3%, and China alone loses approximately 42 billion kilograms of grain annually due to mold growth during storage. Mold contamination causes postharvest losses and mycotoxin exposure, threatening food security. Because visual inspection and laboratory tests usually detect contamination only after fungal growth is established, CAU-China 2026 proposes Fungal Sentinel, a yeast-based early-warning platform for sensing fungal volatile organic compounds (VOCs) before visible mold appears. Using engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a chassis, Fungal Sentinel reconstructs an olfactory sensing pathway by expressing mammalian olfactory receptors that detect mold-associated VOCs such as 1-octen-3-ol. A multi-receptor array improves specificity through VOC fingerprinting, while pathway rewiring with a GPA1/Gaolf chimera and RTP1S enhances signal transmission. VOC exposure triggers mStayGold fluorescence. For deployment, we will integrate stakeholder feedback and develop a low-cost portable fluorescence detector using LED excitation, smartphone imaging, and automated analysis. In the future, additional receptors and machine-learning-assisted pattern recognition could enable fungal species discrimination and broader food safety applications.

Cultural Connection

Grain, as the foundation for a prosperous and stable country, has always been important throughout history. Beijing has a history of over 800 years as the capital city. To ensure the huge food demand in the capital, emperors across dynasties built networks of granaries, laying the groundwork for food safety insurance and cultivating the grain storage culture. Ancient Chinese people used their wisdom and experiences to skillfully prevent food spoilage. For example, the Lumi granary opens windows on the top and air holes below, keeping the air flowing and the temperature steady, so stored grain can be preserved for a long time without becoming moldy. Although the technology of grain storage keeps advancing, molds are still considered a threat that cannot be ignored for food preservation. Globally, approximately 3% of grain lost is caused directly by mold annually, with China losing 42 billion kg in storage, posing threats to food security and storage monitoring. Molds can also produce poisonous mycotoxins like aflatoxins, ochratoxin, and fumonisin, which can cause vomiting, diarrhea, and fever. The danger of mold has never been far away from us, but luckily CAU-China 2026 came up with the idea of Fungal Sentinel, a yeast-based early-warning platform designed to sense fungal volatile organic compounds (VOCs) before visible mold appears. Our project targets the enclosed environment of household small granaries, ensuring the appearance of mold is predictable and lowering the risk of mycotoxins to a minimum. From grain storage culture in ancient times to high technology-based VOC biosensor, Fungal Sentinel carries forward the 800-year-old legacy of Beijing's grain culture into the era of synthetic biology.

Project Description

Current global climate strategies depend heavily on macro-weather reports and satellite imaging, but these frameworks remain blind to subterranean micro-climate crises in local environments. Climate change destabilizes plants by triggering fluctuations in essential stress hormones, including salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), and abscisic acid (ABA). Because regional weather alerts cover broad municipal areas rather than hyper-local realities, individual growers are left decoupled from molecular shifts inside their plants before visible symptoms like wilting appear. To bridge this data gap, we engineered a soil-extract and leaf-fluid biosensor powered by multiple E. coli molecular systems to act as a “ground-up” translator. By analyzing leaf fluid directly, the biosensor detects real-time hormone fluctuations to signal early plant stress. Consultations with home gardeners confirmed the need for hyper-local diagnostic tools, while plant physiology experts guided promoter sensitivity optimization. Feedback from biosecurity policymakers catalyzed a crucial design pivot from live bacterial deployment to a strictly contained, cell-free diagnostic kit. Economic and environmental audits ensured our business model remains affordable and our disposal protocols prevent synthetic DNA contamination in local watersheds. Beyond individual family gardens or small farms, this platform scales into a broader environmental monitoring framework. As non-expert plant lovers collect hyper-local measurements, these decentralized data points aggregate into a real-time, high-resolution network mapping underground conditions and plant health across entire regions. This crowdsourced network bridges the gap between macro-climate data and local realities, empowering everyday growers with actionable molecular insights to save their plants.

In doing so, we weave synthetic biology into the cultural tapestry of modern Guangdong, transforming citizens into stewards of the land, uniting technology with ecology, and ensuring that as our city reaches toward the future, its roots remain deeply connected, protected, and heard.

Project Description

Our iGEM project addresses the severe physical and psychological burden of chronic wounds, a prevalent issue profoundly diminishing the quality of life for Hong Kong's aging population. These non-healing wounds are characterized by a hostile microenvironment defined by persistent bacterial infection, tissue degradation, and, most critically, severe chronic inflammation. The primary driver of this localized chronic inflammation is the constant release of proinflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, and COX-2. To reduce the overproduction of these factors, we targeted Toll-like Receptor 4 (TLR4), which drives their release through the NF-κB signaling pathway. Our objective is to specifically interrupt this cascade—suppressing the overactive chronic response to alleviate suffering while preserving the baseline immune function necessary for natural healing. To achieve this, we are developing a smart hydrogel patch powered by cutting-edge de novo computational protein design. Utilizing deep learning frameworks, we employ RFdiffusion to generate entirely novel structural protein backbones tailored to block TLR4 activation. We then apply ProteinMPNN to assign the precise amino acids for structural stability, followed by AlphaFold3 prediction and Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations to validate our engineered inhibitors against the target pathway. Once validated, these custom de novo proteins will be integrated into a moisture-retaining hydrogel matrix equipped with a dynamic delivery system. To ensure precision therapy, our design incorporates Nitric Oxide (NO) detection—a key biomarker of the wound's hyperinflammatory gaseous environment. Because release is governed by an NO-responsive mechanism, the therapeutic proteins are deployed only when chronic inflammation is actively detected. By intelligently halting the TLR4-mediated cascade exactly when needed, our smart hydrogel breaks the chronic inflammatory cycle and actively promotes tissue repair.

Cultural Connection

Hong Kong has inherited a profound tradition of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and folk topical wound-care culture, rooted in its labour-intensive urban history. For centuries, dock workers and local labourers frequently sustained skin abrasions and chronic external injuries. Local grassroots communities widely adopted herbal plasters as gentle topical interventions. Rather than aggressively excising viable yet impaired tissue, classic TCM wound-healing philosophy emphasizes regulating local pathological disturbances, restraining excessive pathogenic responses, and preserving the body's intrinsic regenerative capacity to enable self-repair—a principle known as “supporting healthy Qi to expel pathogenic factors”. Traditional plasters shelter wounds and modulate the local microenvironment, rather than relying on forced debridement. This mild, regenerative philosophy has become a precious local medical heritage. However, conventional herbal plasters fail to treat modern intractable chronic wounds, including diabetic foot ulcers and pressure sores. Project Description

TEMPO is a modular synthetic timing system composed of three functional layers: an oscillator, a binary counter, and an effector, corresponding to a biological clock, memory, and actuator. The oscillator generates periodic molecular pulses that define discrete units of biological time. These pulses are converted into integrate expression signals and transmitted to the downstream counter. Instead of measuring absolute hours directly, TEMPO counts repeated oscillator cycles, allowing elapsed time to be encoded into stable DNA states. At the core of the system is an expandable integrate-RDF-based binary counter. Each input pulse triggers reversible site-specific DNA recombination, switching an individual bit between two genetic states. By cascading multiple switches, the system can progress through binary states such as 000, 001, 010, 011, and 100, enabling scalable pulse counting and long-term genetic memory. Once a predefined state is reached, a state-specific promoter activates an effector module, enabling functions such as timed protein expression or programmed cell elimination. Mathematical modeling serves as a central bridge between these modules. We characterized oscillator period, amplitude, and recombine output, and directly coupled the simulated oscillator waveform to the binary counter to test whether realistic pulses could reliably drive DNA-state transitions. For the single-bit counter, parameter screening was used to optimize repressor-promoter strength, assess the need for degradation tags, and guide experimental construction. We further compared two multi-bit carry strategies—a feed-forward pulse-based cascade and a free-recombine-mediated cascade—to evaluate scalability. Finally, global sensitivity analysis identified the parameters that most strongly influence oscillation period, switching efficiency, and overall robustness. Modeling also revealed a potential limitation in expression termination, motivating an additional control module for more precise timing. Together, TEMPO transforms time from a passive biological variable into an engineeringable input, providing a quantitative and modular framework for autonomous gene expression, biocontainment, biosensing, and programmable therapeutic systems.

Project Description

Transparent materials are everywhere — in windows, displays, lenses, and laboratory equipment — yet most of them are still made through resource-intensive chemical processes. Global glass production keeps climbing, from about 210 million tonnes in 2022 towards a projected 256 million tonnes by 2030, while recycling rates remain as low as 10–30% in many developing regions. Meanwhile, around 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted in 2022, and uneaten food is linked to 8–10% of global greenhouse gas emissions. Conventional transparent materials, whether inorganic or organic, share the same bottlenecks: petrochemical or mineral feedstocks, high-temperature and high-pressure processing, heavy carbon footprints, and poor biodegradability.

There is another gap hiding in biomanufacturing itself. Today, microbial fermentation mostly stops at basic chemicals; the final step from chemicals to functional materials is still handed over to chemical polymerization. Our project, Suprashell, aims to close that gap with a fully biological synthesis route that converts food waste directly into the building blocks of a new transparent material — no chemical polymerization, no extreme conditions.

The strategy assembles a host and a guest, both made by biology. Route A: Starchy food waste is converted by cyclodextrin glycosyltransferase (CGTase) into beta-cyclodextrin, the "gold standard" host molecule with an optimal macrocyclic cavity and a mature, low-cost industrial supply chain. Route B: Engineered Escherichia coli turns glucose into L-homoserine, a small hydrogen-bonding guest molecule. We rewired the aspartate pathway (glucose → G6P → OAA / fumarate → L-aspartate → L-homoserine), knocked out byproduct routes (delta-ppc, delta-ldhA), boosted NADPH supply through gnd overexpression, introduced feedback-resistant ThrA*, and overexpressed the exporter RhtB to push flux towards L-homoserine.

When host and guest meet, they recognize and nest into each other, forming a supramolecular network that is then shaped by mild hot pressing into a bulk transparent material. Because the assembly relies on dynamic non-covalent bonds, the material can be reprocessed and recycled, closing the loop from food waste back to a useful product under mild, low-carbon conditions. As an iGEM Measurement-track team, we also pioneer a multi-dimensional characterization framework for non-liquid, bulk transparent materials — extending measurement beyond the liquid-phase standards that dominate the field today, with optical properties already validated and mechanical, thermal, and aging evaluations underway. Suprashell is our answer to a simple question: can biology manufacture the transparent materials our daily life depends on, sustainably? We believe iGEM is not an exam but a full year of real research, and we are excited to share our work with NAU-China and teams from all over the world. See you at the Grand Jamboree in Paris.
